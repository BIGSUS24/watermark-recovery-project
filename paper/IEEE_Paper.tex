\begin{abstract}
Consumer editing tools make convincing local image manipulation trivial, yet passive forensic detectors return opaque confidence scores, need annotated training corpora and GPU inference, and cannot restore what was destroyed. Recent learned proactive watermarks (e.g.\ EditGuard, DeepMark) now both localize and recover tampering too, so ``detects and recovers'' is no longer a distinguishing claim. What this paper offers instead is a self-embedding fragile watermark that is deterministic, training-free, and cryptographically auditable: every verification decision is a single keyed HMAC comparison any third party holding the key can recompute and check bit-for-bit. Each $8\times8$ block carries, per channel, a 32-bit keyed authentication tag over its own six most-significant bit-planes and the recovery descriptor it holds, plus a 96-bit compressed descriptor of a spatially distant partner block chosen by a key-seeded single-cycle permutation, all embedded in two least-significant bit-planes. Verification recomputes each tag; a mismatch localizes tampering, and each flagged block is rebuilt from its partner's descriptor or explicitly marked unrecoverable. Switching descriptor-pixel embedding from LSB replacement to LSB shifting raises imperceptibility to 45.479/45.492~dB (SSIM minimum 0.98031, two production variants), essentially closing the gap to a leading published scheme's 45.57~dB. Across 1,792 main-grid tamper trials (four classes, seven ratios, 10--70\%), block-level recall is 1.0000 for three of four classes and 0.999981 (minimum 0.998498) for object-removal-by-inpainting -- the one class where a smooth fill can reproduce a block's own most-significant bits, the predicted failure mode appearing in measurement for the first time, on 22 blocks. Headline localization is pixel-level precision, 0.9614; block-level precision is not reported because the ground-truth rule makes it structurally indistinguishable from 1.0 regardless of detector quality. A 1,802,240-block null condition yields zero false positives. The recoverability rate $\rho$ -- the fraction of tampered blocks whose backup survives -- tracks its structural bound $1-\alpha$ closely, staying 3--6 points above it at every one of the seven measured ratios, refuting an earlier three-point grid's apparent cliff: coverage degrades smoothly, not catastrophically. In-region recovery PSNR stays flat at 29.3--29.8~dB (mean 29.00~dB) across the entire range while whole-image PSNR falls with shrinking coverage -- coverage, not descriptor quality, sets the recovery ceiling, and one-to-one partner mapping still falls short of reference-sharing schemes at high ratios. Every decision traces to a specific hash mismatch rather than a learned score.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The evidentiary value of a digital image rests on an assumption that is increasingly unsafe: that the pixels presented are the pixels captured. Region-level manipulation -- pasting an object from one photograph into another, erasing an object with an inpainting tool, or replacing a cropped region with plausible texture -- is now available in free software and, with diffusion-based generative editors, requires neither skill nor time. The consequences are concrete rather than hypothetical. A radiological study with a lesion removed, a vehicle damage photograph submitted in an insurance claim, a surveillance frame entered into a legal record, or a remote-sensing tile used in an environmental dispute all derive their utility from integrity that the image format itself does not protect.

Two broad families of countermeasures exist. \emph{Passive} (blind) forensics analyses the received image alone, searching for statistical inconsistencies in noise residuals, compression history, colour filter array traces, illumination or resampling artefacts; modern instances of this family are learned, using convolutional or transformer backbones trained on manipulation corpora to output a per-pixel manipulation heat map \cite{zhou2018learning,wu2019mantranet}. \emph{Active} approaches insert authentication information into the image before distribution, so that verification becomes a decision about self-consistency rather than an inference about provenance. Fragile and semi-fragile watermarking belongs to this second family \cite{linchang2000,rey2002survey}.

Even a perfect detector, however, answers only half of the question a practitioner actually asks. Knowing \emph{that} a region of a radiograph was altered, and even knowing precisely \emph{which} pixels were altered, does not restore the diagnostic content that was overwritten. In the passive paradigm, restoration is necessarily a generative act: an inpainting network hallucinates content that is plausible but has no evidentiary relationship to the original. This is the motivating observation of the present work. Detection alone is insufficient, and restoration that invents content is worse than no restoration at all when the artefact is evidence. What is required is a mechanism by which the \emph{original} content -- even a coarse, low-frequency approximation of it -- survives the attack and can be reinstated.

Self-embedding watermarking, introduced in the line of work following Fridrich and Goljan \cite{fridrich1999selfembedding,fridrich1999selfcorrecting}, provides exactly this mechanism: a compressed representation of each region of the image is hidden elsewhere \emph{within the same image}, so that a spatially localized attack destroys the region but not its backup. Our system develops this idea into a complete, reproducible localization-and-recovery pipeline and -- critically -- evaluates the two capabilities \emph{together}, which the literature has tended not to do.

A second motivation is deployment economics and accountability. Learned tamper detectors carry three costs that are frequently understated: they require large annotated manipulation datasets whose bias determines what the model can see; they require accelerator hardware at inference time, which excludes embedded acquisition devices, hospital PACS gateways and field equipment; and their outputs are not explainable in a form that survives adversarial cross-examination. A per-pixel softmax score of 0.78 is not an argument. By contrast, the scheme described here is classical deterministic signal processing: every positive detection is the statement ``the 32-bit keyed tag stored in block $i$ does not equal the tag recomputed from block $i$'s current most-significant content,'' which is a verifiable, repeatable, and auditable fact. There is no training set, no random initialization, no GPU, and no run-to-run variance.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A concrete self-embedding fragile watermarking design that separates \emph{authentication} payload (stored in-block) from \emph{recovery} payload (stored in a distant partner block), with a bit budget that fits entirely within two least-significant bit-planes and therefore bounds embedding distortion analytically.
\item A specific construction avoiding the \emph{tamper coincidence problem} (a block and its own recovery data destroyed together): a key-seeded single-cycle permutation over the block index set, built in cycle notation so bijectivity and the absence of short cycles hold unconditionally, with an enforced minimum-separation repair pass. The distant-mapping principle itself is AuSR3's \cite{ausr3}; the construction is ours.
\item A joint evaluation protocol over four tamper classes at seven tamper ratios with exact, self-generated ground truth, reporting imperceptibility, localization, recoverability rate, and in-region recovery PSNR/SSIM together, so the localization--recovery trade-off is visible rather than implied.
\item An explicit positioning against both classical self-embedding and recent learned proactive watermarking, which now also localizes \emph{and} recovers \cite{editguard,deepmark,recovermark}: the axis of difference is determinism, zero training, zero GPU inference, and full explainability, not accuracy superiority. We state candidly the scheme's core limitation -- fragility to any lossy re-encoding -- and the lossless-archival contexts where that is acceptable.
\item An adversarial security analysis surfacing and fixing two real defects with verified exploits: an unauthenticated recovery descriptor (96 of 128 payload bits unprotected, letting an attacker certify a gutted image as authentic) and a block-mapping seed order leaking spatial structure beyond its stated guarantee (letting an attacker bias a coverage-denial attack without the key). Both fixed at zero payload cost and verified against the exploit that found them.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Robust versus fragile watermarking.} Early watermarking research was dominated by \emph{robustness}: surviving compression, filtering and geometric distortion so ownership could be asserted, canonically Cox \emph{et al.}'s spread-spectrum formulation \cite{cox1997secure}. Authentication inverts the requirement -- the watermark should be \emph{fragile}, failing under modification, because failure is the signal. Wong and Memon \cite{wong2001secret} developed block-wise fragile schemes with per-block signatures in the LSBs, localized and computable without the original image; see \cite{rey2002survey} for a survey of the fragile/semi-fragile design space.

\textbf{Semi-fragile authentication and the JPEG problem.} A purely fragile watermark cannot distinguish a malicious splice from a benign JPEG re-save. Lin and Chang \cite{linchang2000} exploit an invariant DCT-coefficient-pair relationship preserved by quantization but broken by manipulation, establishing the semi-fragile pattern; we cite it to delimit scope, since this system is deliberately fully fragile (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses the extension path).

\textbf{Self-embedding and content recovery.} The decisive step from detection to \emph{recovery} is due to Fridrich and Goljan, embedding a compressed version of each block within the image itself so a tampered region can be approximately reconstructed from surviving data \cite{fridrich1999selfembedding,fridrich1999selfcorrecting}; their block-wise compression, key-dependent distant mapping, and LSB embedding is the direct ancestor of this design. Lin \emph{et al.} \cite{lin2005hierarchical} verify at several granularities simultaneously, informing the neighbourhood post-processing in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Zhang and Wang \cite{zhang2008fragile,zhang2009extensive} and Zhang \emph{et al.} \cite{zhang2011reference} reframe payload allocation so reference information is \emph{shared} across blocks and reconstruction solves a linear system, raising the tolerable tamper ratio above the naive one-to-one limit. Korus and Dziech \cite{korus2013efficient} give a formal rate--distortion analysis of reconstruction quality vs.\ tampering rate. Aminuddin and Ernawan's AuSR series \cite{ausr1,ausr2,ausr3} target the same problem at finer $2\times2$ granularity and name the \emph{tamper coincidence problem} -- a block and its own recovery data damaged together; AuSR3 \cite{ausr3} addresses it by mapping to the most distant available location, the same distant-mapping principle Section~\ref{sec:method} implements as a single-cycle permutation with a minimum-separation repair pass.

\textbf{Security of localized authentication watermarks.} Holliman and Memon \cite{holliman2000counterfeiting} showed that if each block's watermark depends only on its own content, an adversary can collage authentic blocks from several legitimately watermarked images into a \emph{counterfeit} that verifies everywhere; Fridrich \cite{fridrich2002security} analysed localizing-watermark security in the same spirit. The consequence, adopted in Section~\ref{sec:method}: the tag must bind block index and an image-level identifier under a secret key, so a transplanted or relocated block fails verification.

\textbf{Learned manipulation localization.} Passive, learning-based localization has advanced rapidly: two-stream RGB/noise-residual splice localization \cite{zhou2018learning} and self-supervised anomaly-feature forgery localization \cite{wu2019mantranet}. These need no acquisition-device cooperation -- their advantage over any active scheme -- at the cost of annotated training data, GPU inference, non-explainable scores, and distribution-shift sensitivity, which this design avoids. The two families are complementary: active watermarking where the capture pipeline is controllable, passive forensics as the fallback.

\textbf{Learned proactive watermarking.} A separate and more recent line of work embeds a learned watermark specifically so that a trained network can both localize tampering and recover the original content, closing exactly the gap that has traditionally motivated self-embedding. EditGuard \cite{editguard} jointly optimizes an invertible watermarking network for tamper localization and copyright protection, reporting over 95\% localization precision; DeepMark \cite{deepmark} trains a proactive tamper detector and localizer across both images and video; RecoverMark \cite{recovermark} targets joint immunization and recovery specifically for manipulated face imagery. These systems demonstrate that ``detects only, does not recover'' is no longer an accurate characterization of active watermarking as a field. The distinction that survives is not capability but \emph{auditability}: a learned localizer's output is a trained network's inference, not independently recomputable by a third party without the model weights, whereas every decision in the scheme below is a single keyed HMAC comparison that anyone holding the key can recompute and check by hand. We therefore do not claim to detect or recover more accurately than these learned systems; we claim a different, complementary property.

\textbf{Positioning.} Across the self-embedding literature, evaluation is asymmetric: detection papers report localization accuracy, recovery papers report reconstruction PSNR under one attack, and few report both jointly over several tamper classes and ratios against exact ground truth. Supplying that joint characterization for a minimal, training-free construction is this paper's contribution, scoped to the classical, deterministic self-embedding literature specifically (the learned proactive family increasingly reports both together too).

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Notation and Preliminaries}

Let the host image be a grayscale array $I$ of size $M \times N$, 8-bit, $I(x,y) \in \{0,\dots,255\}$; colour images apply the procedure independently per channel. Spatial dimensions are assumed multiples of block size $B=8$; otherwise the image is cropped to the largest block-aligned region rather than padded, since padding would place real blocks' descriptors into discarded phantom blocks. Every corpus image (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) already satisfies this, so no crop occurs in practice.

The image is partitioned into $K = (M/B)\cdot(N/B)$ non-overlapping blocks $\{\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_K\}$ in raster order, $\mathbf{b}_i \in \{0,\dots,255\}^{B\times B}$. Let $c(i) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ denote the pixel-coordinate centroid of block $i$.

Two bit-planes are reserved for embedding. Define the \textbf{MSB projection} operator, which zeroes the two least-significant bits of every pixel:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}) = \left\lfloor \mathbf{b} / 4 \right\rfloor \cdot 4
\label{eq:msb}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{b} \,\text{AND}\, \texttt{0xFC}$. All payload -- both the authentication tag and the recovery descriptor -- is computed from $\mathrm{MSB}(\cdot)$ of the source block, never from the raw block, so embedding cannot alter the quantity that was hashed or compressed. The verifier recomputes the identical quantity from the received image without needing the original, and no iterative fixed-point search is required.

The per-block payload capacity is $2 \times B^2 = 128$ bits, allocated as
\begin{equation}
128 = \underbrace{32}_{\text{tag } a_i} + \underbrace{96}_{\text{descriptor } r_j,\ j=m^{-1}(i)}
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
so block $i$ carries its \emph{own} authentication tag and \emph{another} block's recovery descriptor. The 32-bit tag yields a per-block false-acceptance probability of $2^{-32}\approx 2.3\times10^{-10}$, negligible over the $K=4096$ blocks of a $512\times512$ image. \textbf{This 32+96 budget is per channel, not per block overall}: a colour image applies the full construction independently on each of its three channels, so a colour block carries three independent 32-bit tags and three independent 96-bit descriptors (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), not one 128-bit payload shared across channels; Table~\ref{tab:falseaccept} and Section~\ref{sec:results} give the resulting multi-channel false-accept probability explicitly rather than leaving 128 bits/block to be misread as the whole-pixel figure.

\subsection{Recovery Descriptor Construction}

For each block $i$, a 96-bit descriptor $r_i$ compressing the visual content of $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)$ is computed. Three variants are specified; Variants A and C form the main experimental grid, Variant B is evaluated only as an ablation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\textbf{Variant A (DCT, primary).} Let $\mathbf{C}_i = \mathrm{DCT2}(\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i) - 128)$ be the level-shifted 2-D type-II DCT of the block. Let $\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(64)$ be the zig-zag scan order; retain the first $L=12$ coefficients (DC plus eleven lowest-frequency AC terms). Each retained coefficient is quantized to a \emph{uniform} 8 signed bits,
\begin{equation}
\hat{q}_{i,\ell} = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{C_i(\sigma(\ell))}{\Delta_\ell}\right), -128, 127\right),\; \ell=1,\dots,12,
\label{eq:quant}
\end{equation}
so $L\times8 = 12\times8 = 96$ bits exactly, with round-half-even rounding. The decreasing-precision intent of natural-image DCT coding is carried entirely by the per-band step size $\Delta_\ell$ rather than by a decreasing bit allocation: $\Delta_\ell$ is taken from the JPEG quality-50 luminance quantization table in zig-zag order, with the DC entry reduced to 8 so that the DC term -- which carries the most energy -- retains the finest quantization step. An increasing $\Delta_\ell$ is an equivalent, and simpler to implement, way of expressing a decreasing effective bit allocation. Concatenating the 12 signed bytes in scan order gives $r_i \in \{0,1\}^{96}$; two's-complement byte representation makes packing and sign handling a single array view, with no explicit sign bit.

\emph{No retained coefficient can clip.} With an orthonormal 2-D DCT, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $|C_i(u,v)| \le 128B$ for an 8-bit input block of size $B$; for $B=8$ this is $1024$. The DC step of $8$ gives $1024/8=128$, exactly filling the signed 8-bit range; every AC step is at least $10$, giving $1024/10=102.4<127$. No retained coefficient can therefore clip for any 8-bit input, for any $B=8$ block -- the $\mathrm{clip}(\cdot)$ in \eqref{eq:quant} is a defensive bound only. Reconstruction dequantizes, zero-pads coefficients $\ell>L$, applies the inverse DCT, and re-adds the level shift:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_i = \mathrm{clip}\big(\mathrm{IDCT2}(\mathcal{Z}(\hat{q}_i \odot \Delta)) + 128,\ 0,\ 255\big)
\label{eq:idct}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Z}(\cdot)$ places values into an $8\times8$ zero array at the zig-zag positions.

\textbf{Variant B (mean-pooled, low-complexity).} The $8\times8$ block is reduced to a $4\times4$ array of $2\times2$ means,
\begin{equation}
\mu_i(u,v) = \left\lfloor \frac{1}{4}\sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1} \mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)(2u+p, 2v+q) \right\rfloor
\label{eq:meanpool}
\end{equation}
for $0 \le u,v \le 3$, each quantized to 6 bits by discarding the two least-significant bits, $\hat{\mu}_i = \lfloor \mu_i/4 \rfloor$, giving exactly $16\times6=96$ bits. Reconstruction upsamples $4\hat{\mu}_i+2$ to $8\times8$. Variant B needs no transform -- only integer shifts -- and targets microcontroller-class deployment, but measurably trails A in reconstruction fidelity (Section~\ref{sec:results}); it is retained only as an ablation, not run in the main grid.

\textbf{Variant C (adaptive DCT, main-grid default).} Variant C spends the same 96-bit budget as Variant A on the same DCT coefficients, but chooses per block between two candidate bit-allocation tables rather than committing to one fixed allocation for every block. One bit of the 96 records which table was used; both candidate quantizations are reconstructed (dequantize, IDCT, clip, project to MSB) and compared against the true target block in \emph{pixel} space -- not coefficient space, since the reconstruction path is non-linear -- and the table giving lower squared pixel error is kept. This makes the rate allocation locally optimal over the two candidates rather than merely a reasonable fixed choice, at the cost of one bit of descriptor budget and one extra trial reconstruction per block at embed time; decode-time cost is unchanged, since the mode bit says which table to invert. Measured, paired by (image, tamper class, ratio, key, seed) on the full grid, Variant C's in-region recovery gain over Variant A has mean $+2.63$~dB, worst case $+1.40$~dB (\emph{tiffany}), best case $+3.83$~dB (\emph{kodim08}), with zero images regressing.

In all three variants, the descriptor is a function of $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)$ only, so the two-LSB uncertainty of the recovered block is inherently bounded.

\subsection{Keyed Authentication Tag}

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be SHA-256 \cite{fips180-4} and $K_s$ a secret key. Let $\mathrm{ID}$ be an image-level identifier (nonce, capture timestamp, or device serial) stored alongside the image. The authentication tag of block $i$ is
\begin{equation}
a_i = \mathrm{Trunc}_{32}\Big(\mathrm{HMAC}_{K_s}\big(\mathrm{ID} \,\Vert\, i \,\Vert\, \mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i) \,\Vert\, r_{m^{-1}(i)}\big)\Big)
\label{eq:hmac}
\end{equation}
where $\Vert$ denotes concatenation, $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)$ is serialized in raster order, and $r_{m^{-1}(i)}$ is the recovery descriptor block $i$ itself physically carries (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}). Four properties matter: dependence on $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)$ means embedding into LSBs does not invalidate the tag; dependence on $i$ and $\mathrm{ID}$ defends against block-wise collage counterfeiting \cite{holliman2000counterfeiting}; dependence on $r_{m^{-1}(i)}$ protects the carried payload too; and dependence on $K_s$ means an adversary who edits content cannot forge a consistent authentication layer. This is not precaution for its own sake: an unkeyed, content-only hash is exactly what a published attack \cite{selfembed_attack_arxiv1812} breaks in a similar scheme, and its recommended countermeasures -- keyed hashing and block interdependence -- are exactly the two properties built into \eqref{eq:hmac} and the mapping of Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\textbf{Found-and-fixed: the recovery descriptor was unauthenticated.} An earlier version of \eqref{eq:hmac} bound the tag only to $\mathrm{ID}$, $i$, and $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)$, omitting $r_{m^{-1}(i)}$ -- leaving 96 of every block's 128 stored bits (three quarters of the descriptor payload) with no integrity protection. Verified exploit: flipping the two LSBs of pixels 16--63 in every block destroys every recovery descriptor at 40.29~dB PSNR, yet the old detector flagged 0 of 4096 blocks, certifying a gutted image as authentic and reconstructing every subsequently-flagged block from attacker-chosen bits. The fix costs zero payload bits -- the tag message now also covers the carried descriptor, as in \eqref{eq:hmac}, since the embedder already held that array -- and the on-disk magic was bumped (\texttt{WGT1}$\to$\texttt{WGT2}) so old watermarks fail loudly against the fixed verifier. Post-fix, the identical attack flags 4096 of 4096 blocks. Imperceptibility is unchanged, since the fix changes only what is hashed, not what is embedded.

\subsection{Key-Seeded Block Mapping}

The mapping $m:\{1,\dots,K\}\to\{1,\dots,K\}$ determines which block stores which recovery descriptor: $r_i$ is embedded in block $m(i)$. It must satisfy:

\textbf{(R1) Bijectivity} -- $m$ is a permutation, so every 96-bit slot is exactly filled.

\textbf{(R2) Spatial separation} -- $\lVert c(i)-c(m(i))\rVert_2 \ge d_{\min}$ for all $i$, so a localized attack of diameter smaller than $d_{\min}$ cannot cover both a block and the block holding its descriptor. We set $d_{\min} = \tfrac{1}{4}\min(M,N)$.

\textbf{(R3) Absence of short cycles} -- $m$ must be a \emph{single cycle} of length $K$, forbidding fixed points and mutual pairs $\{i, m(i)\}$ with $m(m(i))=i$ by construction. This eliminates the two-block instance of the \emph{tamper coincidence problem} \cite{ausr3} -- the situation in which one attack destroys both a block and the block holding its own recovery data.

The classical construction $m(i) = ((k\cdot i + t)\bmod K) + 1$, $\gcd(k,K)=1$, satisfies (R1) cheaply but has weak security and does not guarantee (R2). Our primary construction: (1) seed a CSPRNG with $K_s \Vert \mathrm{ID}$; (2) draw a uniformly random cyclic permutation via Fisher--Yates, defining $m$ directly as a single $K$-cycle, satisfying (R1) and (R3) exactly; (3) apply a bounded, monotone separation-repair pass that transposes cycle successors until (R2) holds everywhere, capped and deterministically retried on failure, reporting both the requested separation $d_{\min,\text{requested}}$ and the value actually achieved, $d_{\min,\text{achieved}}$. Both sender and verifier regenerate $m$ from $(K_s,\mathrm{ID},K)$; the mapping itself is never transmitted.

\textbf{Found-and-fixed: the seed order gave away more than $d_{\min}$.} An earlier version seeded step (2) with a fixed quadrant interleave (splitting blocks by grid quadrant, shuffling each independently, interleaving diagonally-opposite quadrants) adopted only to make step (3)'s repair converge faster. Measured, this leaked structure beyond the stated $d_{\min}$ constraint: a block's partner fell diagonally opposite 47.75\% of the time against a 29.32\% separation-only baseline -- never threatening authentication (the mapping is not the secret \eqref{eq:hmac} protects), but letting an attacker who knows only the published algorithm, with no key, bias a coverage-denial attack toward a target region and its predictable diagonal partner. The optimisation proved unnecessary: a flat keyed shuffle violates (R2) on only 15\% of cycle edges, and the same monotone repair pass in step (3) clears every violation in the same $\approx0.1$s. It was removed; the map now measures 30.08\% diagonal-opposite, statistically indistinguishable from the 29.32\% baseline. Not free: Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies the cost in $\rho$, roughly 1.5 points at every tested ratio -- the price of closing that exposure.

\subsection{Embedding Procedure}

Given $I$, $K_s$, $\mathrm{ID}$, the watermarked image $I_w$ is produced in one pass: (1) partition $I$ and compute $\mathbf{b}_i^{\mathrm{msb}}=\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}_i)$; (2) compute $r_i$ and $a_i$ from $\mathbf{b}_i^{\mathrm{msb}}$; (3) generate $m$ and $m^{-1}$; (4) form payload $p_i = a_i \,\Vert\, r_{m^{-1}(i)}$; (5) write $p_i$ into the two LSBs of $\mathbf{b}_i^{\mathrm{msb}}$.

\textbf{Distortion at a full-entropy payload.} Treating the two embedded LSBs as independent and uniform on $\{0,1,2,3\}$ -- the case of a full-entropy payload -- the per-pixel error satisfies $\mathbb{E}[e^2] = 2\,\mathrm{Var}(U\{0,3\}) = 2.5$, giving
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSNR}_{U} = 10\log_{10}\frac{255^2}{2.5} \approx 44.15\ \text{dB}.
\label{eq:psnrmax}
\end{equation}
This is a reference point, not an upper bound: it is the PSNR realized when the embedded payload's pair values are exactly uniform, and neither descriptor variant used in this paper is exactly uniform (Section~\ref{sec:results} derives and measures each variant's actual, opposite-signed deviation from $\mathrm{PSNR}_U$). It remains useful as a correctness check on the implementation, since a measured PSNR far from $\mathrm{PSNR}_U$ on a full-entropy synthetic payload specifically indicates a projection or serialization defect rather than genuine content statistics.

\subsection{Tamper Detection and Mask Generation}

Given a received image $I'$, the same key and $\mathrm{ID}$: (1) extract stored tag $\hat{a}_i$ and stored descriptor $\hat{r}_{m^{-1}(i)}$ from each block's 128 LSBs; (2) recompute $a'_i$ from $\mathrm{MSB}(\mathbf{b}'_i)$ via \eqref{eq:hmac}; (3) form the raw indicator
\begin{equation}
d_i = \begin{cases} 1, & \hat{a}_i \ne a'_i \\ 0, & \hat{a}_i = a'_i \end{cases}
\label{eq:rawindicator}
\end{equation}
(4) apply a neighbourhood refinement pass, correcting isolated positives (likely bit-error false alarms) and isolated negatives (a block whose $\mathrm{MSB}$ content happened to survive despite all its neighbours failing):
\begin{equation}
d^{\ast}_i = \begin{cases} 0, & d_i=1 \text{ and } S_i=0 \\ 1, & d_i=0 \text{ and } S_i \ge \theta_i \\ d_i, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\label{eq:refined}
\end{equation}
where $S_i = \sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_8(i)} d_k$, $|\mathcal{N}_8(i)|$ is the number of blocks actually adjacent to $i$ (8 in the interior, 5 on an edge, 3 at a corner), and the fill threshold is scaled to the valid-neighbour count, $\theta_i = \left\lceil \tau\cdot|\mathcal{N}_8(i)|/8 \right\rceil$ with $\tau=7$. A fixed threshold of 7 would make border and corner blocks impossible to fill -- a corner block has only 3 valid neighbours, so $S_i\le3<7$ always -- silently exempting the image border from the fill rule; scaling by $|\mathcal{N}_8(i)|/8$ keeps the fill rule proportionally identical everywhere.

\textbf{The isolated-positive branch of \eqref{eq:refined} is disabled by default, on measured grounds.} It exists to suppress bit-error false alarms, but that rationale does not survive contact with an exact keyed comparison: the null condition ($1{,}802{,}240$ block verifications) produced identically zero false positives, so the branch cannot remove one -- only a true positive. It also creates a blind spot: a single-block tamper ($S_i=0$) is erased entirely, and at $B=8$ that is 64 pixels, ample to alter a digit on a financial instrument. We therefore retain the isolated-\emph{negative} fill (closes holes inside a tampered region) and disable the isolated-positive clear by default, exposed as an explicit parameter (\texttt{clear\_isolated}) rather than hard-coded.

The isolated-negative fill is not free, and we disclose its cost. Unlike the positive clear, which can only \emph{remove} a detection, the negative fill can \emph{manufacture} one, flipping a block positive because its neighbours are, regardless of its own tag comparison. Across the full grid this happened to five blocks total out of 7{,}534{,}263 checks (\texttt{n\_refinement\_flagged}~=~5): each was authentic on its own terms, yet the recovery stage overwrote it, since a flagged block is treated as tampered regardless of how it came to be flagged -- recovery irreversibly overwrites, so a manufactured false positive here means verified-authentic content is destroyed by the recovery stage itself. Neighbourhood smoothing inherited from soft-decision detectors is not automatically appropriate to a hard-decision cryptographic one, where the false-alarm rate it exists to suppress is provably zero.

Then (5) expand to the pixel-level mask
\begin{equation}
T(x,y) = d^{\ast}_{\beta(x,y)}, \quad \beta(x,y) = \left\lfloor\frac{y}{B}\right\rfloor\cdot\frac{N}{B} + \left\lfloor\frac{x}{B}\right\rfloor + 1
\label{eq:maskexpand}
\end{equation}
Localization resolution is intrinsically $B\times B$.

\subsection{Content Recovery}

Recovery proceeds over the flagged set $\mathcal{T}=\{i : d^{\ast}_i=1\}$. Block $i$'s descriptor resides in block $m(i)$, so recovering $i$ requires $m(i)$ to be authentic:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{b}}_i = \begin{cases}
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_i(\hat{r}_i \text{ from } \mathbf{b}'_{m(i)}), & i\in\mathcal{T},\ d^{\ast}_{m(i)}=0 \\
\text{unrecoverable}, & i\in\mathcal{T},\ d^{\ast}_{m(i)}=1 \\
\mathbf{b}'_i, & i \notin \mathcal{T}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
Recovered blocks have their two LSBs zeroed, marking them as reconstructed rather than authentic -- a restored region must never re-authenticate as original. Let $\mathcal{U}=\{i\in\mathcal{T} : d^{\ast}_{m(i)}=1\}$ denote blocks lost to the \emph{tamper coincidence problem} \cite{ausr3} -- a flagged block whose designated partner is itself flagged; the \textbf{recoverability rate} is
\begin{equation}
\rho = 1 - \frac{|\mathcal{U}|}{|\mathcal{T}|}
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Aminuddin \emph{et al.} \cite{tcbr_tcbd} independently name and formalize this same phenomenon as the Tamper Coincidence Block Rate (TCBR) and Tamper Coincidence Block Detection (TCBD): TCBR is defined, like $\rho$ here, as the fraction of tampered blocks whose recovery data survives, so $\rho \equiv \mathrm{TCBR}$ under Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho}, and $|\mathcal{U}|/|\mathcal{T}| = 1-\mathrm{TCBR}$ is their TCBD. We adopt our own notation for derivational continuity with $\mathcal{U}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ above, not because the quantity differs. For a uniformly random mapping and a tampered fraction $\alpha$, $\rho \approx 1-\alpha$; constraint (R2) forces $\rho=1$ exactly for attacks more compact than $d_{\min}$, and (R3) removes the short-cycle instance of the tamper coincidence problem for dispersed attacks. The mapping design, not the descriptor design, sets the recovery-coverage ceiling.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

Embedding and verification are each $O(MN)$: one HMAC evaluation and one $8\times8$ DCT (or sixteen $2\times2$ means) per block, plus $O(K)$ for mapping generation. No iteration, optimization, or training is involved. The reference implementation (NumPy + OpenCV) processes a $512\times512$ grayscale image end-to-end in tens of milliseconds on a commodity CPU, with no accelerator.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The system comprises four decoupled stages so the tamper simulator and the detector share no state.

\textbf{Stage 1 -- Embedding pipeline.} Block Partitioner $\rightarrow$ MSB Projector \eqref{eq:msb} $\rightarrow$ \{Descriptor Encoder, Authentication Tag Generator\} $\rightarrow$ Mapping Generator (CSPRNG, single-cycle permutation, separation repair) $\rightarrow$ Payload Assembler $\rightarrow$ Dual-LSB Embedder $\rightarrow$ watermarked image $I_w$ (lossless PNG/TIFF) plus side information $\mathrm{ID}$. The key never leaves the signer.

\textbf{Stage 2 -- Tamper simulation harness (evaluation only).} Applies a parameterized attack at target area ratio $\alpha$ and emits both the tampered image $I'$ and the exact ground-truth mask $G$ (written by the harness itself, hence exact rather than annotated). No access to $K_s$, $m$, or watermark internals.

\textbf{Stage 3 -- Detection and recovery pipeline.} Block Partitioner $\rightarrow$ Payload Extractor $\rightarrow$ Mapping Regeneration $\rightarrow$ Tag Comparison \eqref{eq:rawindicator} $\rightarrow$ Mask Refinement \eqref{eq:refined} $\rightarrow$ Pixel Mask Expansion \eqref{eq:maskexpand} $\rightarrow$ Recovery Engine \eqref{eq:recovery}. Outputs: predicted mask $T$, recovered image $I_r$, unrecoverable set $\mathcal{U}$, recoverability rate $\rho$, and a per-block audit log -- for every block, the stored tag, the recomputed tag, and its partner block's index, plus, for each unrecoverable block, an explicit refusal reason -- the output is a chain of verifiable comparisons, not a score.

\textbf{Stage 4 -- Evaluation module.} Computes imperceptibility on $(I,I_w)$, confusion-matrix metrics on $(T,G)$ at pixel and block granularity, and recovery metrics on $(I,I_r)$ restricted to the support of $G$, aggregated over the image set and attack grid. All randomness is seeded, so the full experimental grid is bit-reproducible.

Stage 1 is intended to run at or near acquisition (camera firmware, scanner driver, DICOM ingest gateway); Stage 3 runs at any later verification point, requiring only the image, the key, and $\mathrm{ID}$ -- no original, no reference database, no network call, no model weights.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Image Corpus}

Because ground truth is generated by our own tamper harness, no annotated forgery dataset is required. The corpus is 32 colour images, 8~bpp per channel: 8 USC-SIPI Miscellaneous-set images \cite{uscsipi} at $512\times512$, and 24 Kodak PCD0992 images, of which 18 are $768\times512$ (landscape) and 6 are $512\times768$ (portrait) -- not a uniform Kodak dimension. All images are stored losslessly as PNG and SHA-256 pinned in a committed manifest, so any silent change to the corpus between runs is a hard error rather than a silent drift. Any lossy stage would destroy the watermark by design.

Provenance: the USC-SIPI and Kodak-PCD0992 sets as redistributed by the AuSR reference authors (github.com/girfa/ColorImageDatasets); original sources USC-SIPI (sipi.usc.edu) and Eastman Kodak (mirrored at r0k.us/graphics/kodak). USC-SIPI no longer distributes two of the eight images in this set (\emph{Lena} and \emph{Tiffany}) from its own archive, which makes a byte-identical-to-the-official-archive claim impossible regardless of source; using the same redistributed corpus the AuSR series \cite{ausr1,ausr2,ausr3} reports on has the additional benefit that our images are the same bytes their published per-image tables are keyed to. USC-SIPI is licensed for research/education use with attribution; Kodak PCD0992 was released by Eastman Kodak for unrestricted use; neither is redistributed here commercially.

\subsection{Tamper Simulation Protocol}

Four attack classes, each at a nominal target tamper-area ratio $\alpha\in\{10\%,20\%,\dots,70\%\}$ -- seven points, not the three coarser buckets an earlier grid used, so a real trend in $\rho(\alpha)$ cannot hide between sparse samples -- with randomized shapes and positions; the achieved area is reported alongside each row rather than assumed equal to $\alpha$. The classes: (i)~\textbf{copy-paste splicing} from a different watermarked image, where index/ID-binding of \eqref{eq:hmac} is load-bearing against \cite{holliman2000counterfeiting}; (ii)~\textbf{object removal by inpainting} (OpenCV Telea/Navier--Stokes), historically the hardest case for recall since a smooth fill can leave MSB planes numerically unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:results}); (iii)~\textbf{crop-and-refill} via mirrored/tiled neighbouring texture; (iv)~\textbf{random block corruption}, a sanity control expected near-perfect. A \textbf{null condition} ($\alpha=0$) over all 32 images measures the false-positive rate, which must be identically zero given the deterministic construction.

\subsection{Metrics}

Following the transparency / tamper-detection / content-recovery evaluation axes established by the field's survey literature \cite{eurasip2019survey}, we report all three explicitly. Imperceptibility (transparency): PSNR and SSIM \cite{wang2004image} between $I$ and $I_w$. Localization (tamper detection): precision, recall, F1, IoU between predicted mask $T$ and ground truth $G$, at both pixel and block granularity. Recovery (content recovery): PSNR/SSIM computed only inside the tampered support $\Omega=\{(x,y):G(x,y)=1\}$, reported alongside the recoverability rate $\rho$ from \eqref{eq:rho}, since excluding unrecoverable blocks inflates in-region PSNR if $\rho$ is not disclosed jointly.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}

Implemented in Python 3 (NumPy, OpenCV for block DCT and inpainting, \texttt{hashlib}/\texttt{hmac} for \eqref{eq:hmac}) -- no machine learning framework, no trained parameters. Free parameters $B$, descriptor variant, $d_{\min}$, $\tau$ are fixed a priori; all stochastic elements are seeded, so the full grid is bit-reproducible, single-threaded, no GPU. Colour images use one shared block mapping $m$ across channels, with independent per-channel tags/descriptors and a channel-OR detection mask -- a tamper touching one channel flags all three, at the cost of recovery overwriting untouched channels too. The block-level ground truth is an OR-reduction (tampered if the region covers \emph{any} pixel of a block), matching the detector's own quantization. SSIM uses \texttt{data\_range=255}, scikit-image's default $7\times7$ uniform window, and colour SSIM is the mean of per-channel SSIM.

\textbf{LSB shifting closes the gap to AuSR1.} Equation~\eqref{eq:psnrmax}'s 44.15~dB is the reference PSNR for a full-entropy payload under \emph{plain replacement}. AuSR1-style \emph{LSB shifting} moves a pixel to the nearest value whose low two bits already equal the desired payload instead, cutting expected squared error from 2.5 to 1.5. The earlier version of this design could not use shifting: a shift can cross into an adjacent quantization window and change bit~2, inside the six MSB planes \eqref{eq:hmac} authenticates, so a shifted \emph{tag} pixel would make the verifier hash a different value than the embedder signed. The fix shifts only the 48 \emph{descriptor}-carrying pixels per block, never the 16 \emph{tag} pixels: descriptor target bits come from another block's already-fixed descriptor, so shifting them first and folding the result into the MSB content \emph{before} hashing is safe; tag bits are computed \emph{from} that folded content, so letting them shift would make the hash depend on its own output. The mixed-mode full-entropy reference point is $\mathrm{E}[e^2] = (16\cdot2.5 + 48\cdot1.5)/64 = 1.75$, giving 45.70~dB -- within 0.2~dB of the measured 45.479~dB (Variant A) / 45.492~dB (Variant C), SSIM minimum 0.98031 (Table~\ref{tab:imperceptibility}), against AuSR1's published 45.57~dB / SSIM 0.9972 / precision 0.9943. The null-condition zero-false-positive result confirms the fix did not reopen the failure mode it avoids.

\textbf{SSIM remains genuinely content-dependent.} SSIM's structure and contrast terms are normalized by local variance, so a fixed-variance embedding perturbation is comparatively larger on smooth, low-variance content than on strongly textured content. This was checked against a windowing artefact and ruled out: scikit-image's default $7\times7$ uniform window, the Wang \emph{et al.} \cite{wang2004image} $11\times11$ Gaussian window, and a Gaussian window applied to luma agree to within about $0.001$ on this corpus. Variants A and C remain close to each other in imperceptibility, since both are DCT descriptors differing mainly in per-block quantization-mode selection rather than bit count or location (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsubsection{Localization Behaviour}

\textbf{Block-level precision, footnote.}\footnote{Ground truth at block granularity is the OR-reduction of the pixel-level tamper region -- a block is tampered if the region covers \emph{any} pixel of it (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) -- so any block the detector correctly flags necessarily contains at least one ground-truth-tampered pixel; a block-level false positive is structurally impossible under this rule, and a detector that flagged the entire image regardless of content would also score 1.000. Measured block-level precision is 1.000000 with zero variance across every grid row, because there is nothing left to vary. We do not headline it for exactly this reason.} The headline localization figure is instead \textbf{pixel-level precision}, scored against the exact ground-truth mask rather than the block grid, which genuinely varies: mean \textbf{0.9614} across the full seven-ratio main grid. The shortfall from 1.0 is 8$\times$8 block quantization, stated plainly: a flagged block is reported whole even when only part of it was altered, so a partially-covered flagged block contributes clean pixels that score as false positives at pixel granularity though the block itself was correctly flagged, and this cannot improve without a finer grid. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} quantifies the resolution/payload tension directly: reducing $B$ from 8 to 4 raises pixel-level precision and IoU from 0.9527 to 0.9794 (+2.7 points) on the USC-SIPI subset -- at the authentication cost quantified in Table~\ref{tab:falseaccept} above. The central resolution/payload tension of block-based self-embedding is addressed in prior work by verifying at multiple granularities simultaneously \cite{lin2005hierarchical}.

\textbf{Recall, and the one class that misses.} For a block whose content changed in any of bit-planes 2--7, the tag-mismatch probability is $1-2^{-32}$, so any recall deficit isolates to a block the attack wrote to without altering its six MSB planes -- characteristically a smooth inpainting fill over already-flat content. Binding $r_{m^{-1}(i)}$ into the authenticated message (Eq.~\eqref{eq:hmac}) closes most of this surface: such a tamper still perturbs the block's LSBs, which now fall inside the authenticated message. Measured block recall on the seven-ratio main grid (1,792 tamper rows): splice, crop\_refill and noise are each exactly \textbf{1.000000}; \textbf{object removal by inpainting is the sole exception}, at \textbf{0.999981} (minimum 0.998498), with \textbf{22 MSB-preserved-miss blocks} against zero on an earlier, smaller grid. This is not a regression -- it is the fix's own predicted residual, appearing in measurement for the first time because a 896-trial inpainting grid is the first dense enough to sample it: at the boundary of a removal region, a smooth fill can, rarely, leave a block's full byte values -- MSB content \emph{and} its two stored LSB payload bits -- completely unchanged even though ground truth marks the block as inside the nominal tamper region; with nothing written to that block, its tag cannot fail. An intrinsic, bounded residual of content-preserving edits at a mask boundary, not a reopened security gap.

Table~\ref{tab:null} reports the executed check: zero false-positive blocks across all $1{,}802{,}240$ independent block verifications (32 images $\times$ 2 variants $\times$ 5 keys), and a rule-of-three 95\% upper bound of $1.66\times10^{-6}$ on the per-block \emph{false-positive} (Type~I) rate. This bound says nothing about the per-block \emph{false-accept} (Type~II) rate: the null condition contains zero tampered blocks by construction, so the $2^{-32}$ false-accept probability (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}) remains a property of \eqref{eq:hmac}'s output width, not something this experiment measures. Nor are these $1.8\times10^{6}$ evaluations independent Bernoulli trials: the received image is the identical in-memory array the embedder returned, so a false positive here would require \eqref{eq:hmac} to disagree with itself on identical input within a single process -- a determinism failure to detect, not a probabilistic event to bound. The honest description is a \textbf{determinism and implementation-consistency check} across images, variants and keys: a content-dependent-but-key-independent bug would reproduce identically across all five keys, whereas a genuine cryptographic property would not correlate with key choice at all. No such bug was observed. Disk round-tripping is not exercised here, so lossless save/reload is a separate, uncovered property.

\textbf{The recoverability curve does not collapse.} $\rho$ is governed by the mapping: $\rho\approx1$ below $d_{\min}$, $\rho\approx1-\alpha$ for dispersed attacks (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho}). An earlier three-point grid ($\alpha\in\{10\%,25\%,50\%\}$) was too coarse to distinguish a smooth decline from a cliff. The seven-point grid (Table~\ref{tab:rhoalpha}) resolves this: $\rho$ is monotone and near-linear across 10--70\%, no knee, sitting \textbf{3--6 points above} its own bound $1-\alpha$ at every ratio -- consistently, not occasionally, better than the mapping's worst case; not, and we do not claim, parity with reference-sharing schemes at high $\alpha$ (below). The two highest nominal ratios are nominal only: the region generator cannot reliably place one compact region over 60--70\% of a frame, so 0.60 achieves $0.573\pm0.046$ and 0.70 achieves $0.653\pm0.064$ -- read as ``targeted,'' not ``delivered,'' and we do not present 0.70 as though 70\% were actually tampered.

\textbf{The block-mapping structural-leak fix (Section~\ref{sec:method}) has a measured, quantified recoverability cost, reported as the price of the security property, not a regression.} Before removing the quadrant-interleave seed order, the identical measurement gave $\rho$ roughly 1.5 points higher at every ratio than the flat keyed shuffle now used, because the leaky interleave biased a block's partner toward the diagonally opposite (and typically farther, hence safer) quadrant. That higher $\rho$ was partly an artefact an attacker who knew only the published algorithm, with no key, could exploit in the opposite direction to make a coverage-denial attack \emph{more} reliable; 1.5 points of $\rho$ is the measured price of closing that exposure.

\textbf{In-region recovery quality is flat -- this confirms, not just illustrates, that coverage sets the ceiling, not descriptor fidelity.} PSNR$_\Omega$ (Table~\ref{tab:rhoalpha}, rightmost column) stays in a narrow \textbf{29.3--29.8~dB} band across the entire $\alpha=10\%$--$70\%$ range (mean \textbf{29.00~dB} over every row of the main grid), while $\rho$ falls by a factor of 2.7 over the same range. Descriptor fidelity is a property of a single reconstructed block and is essentially independent of how many blocks needed reconstruction; whole-image quality is a property of the whole picture and depends on \emph{how much} of it had to be reconstructed, which is exactly what $\rho$ and $\alpha$ jointly set. Seven flat points, not one or two, is what makes this a confirmed structural finding rather than an anecdote.

A one-to-one partner mapping still fails \emph{all-or-nothing} at the block level: the tamper coincidence problem removes a whole block's worth of recovery at once, whereas reference-sharing and fountain-coded allocations \cite{zhang2011reference,korus2013efficient} spread each block's recovery information across many partners, degrading gracefully instead -- which is why Korus and Dziech report 37~dB whole-image reconstruction at 50\% damage against our measured 17.4~dB whole-image unmarked at the same ratio (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). We state this gap plainly and do not imply parity: closing it (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}, Future Scope item~1) remains the highest-value single upgrade to this system, distinct from and not remedied by the $\rho$-vs-bound finding above, which concerns how well the current mapping performs against \emph{its own} structural limit, not against a different allocation strategy's limit.

\textbf{Marking convention.} Our default output marks unrecoverable blocks explicitly rather than fabricating content -- the honest choice, at a whole-image PSNR cost relative to leaving those pixels at their received value: at $\alpha=50\%$, marked whole-image PSNR is 12.4~dB against 17.4~dB unmarked (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). Published recovery figures in the literature are, as best we can determine, whole-image and unmarked, so we report both: a \emph{marked} figure describing actual deployed output, and an \emph{unmarked} figure for literature comparability. Neither should be confused with the stricter \emph{in-region} figure above.

\textbf{Fidelity} (PSNR$_\Omega$, computed in-region on recovered blocks only) is governed by the descriptor rate: 96 bits for 64 pixels (1.5~bpp), of which Variant A's twelve-coefficient DCT truncation (Section~\ref{sec:method}) discards all high-frequency content. Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows a representative end-to-end example. Reconstruction error tracks the magnitude of the high-frequency detail the descriptor discards, so twelve low-frequency DCT coefficients cannot represent fine texture. The flat 29.3--29.8~dB band (Table~\ref{tab:rhoalpha}) is the honestly-characterized outcome -- a faithful \emph{low-frequency} reconstruction, visibly softer than its surroundings, with fine texture and sharp edges not returning. For the target applications (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) this is the correct trade: a verifier needs to know what was there approximately and verifiably, not be shown a sharp fabrication.

Reporting $\rho$ and PSNR$_\Omega$ together (Table~\ref{tab:rhoalpha}) is deliberate: PSNR$_\Omega$ must never be reported without $\rho$ alongside it, since a reader cannot otherwise tell whether a healthy in-region figure reflects strong reconstruction or simply a small, easy-to-recover flagged set. Here it is the stronger finding: $\rho$ falls by a factor of 2.7 over the grid while PSNR$_\Omega$ moves by under 0.5~dB, direct evidence that whole-image recovery quality is governed by coverage, not by descriptor fidelity.

\subsection{Comparison, Explainability, and Limitations}

\subsubsection{Comparison to Learned Methods}

``Detects and recovers'' is no longer a distinguishing claim: EditGuard \cite{editguard} and DeepMark \cite{deepmark} both localize and recover with a trained network, and RecoverMark \cite{recovermark} targets recovery specifically. A direct numeric comparison against passive learned localizers \cite{zhou2018learning,wu2019mantranet} is not offered either, since those localize manipulation in images never seen before embedding, which this system cannot do at all. The honest axis of difference against the learned \emph{proactive} family is not capability but \textbf{auditability}: every decision here is a single recomputed HMAC comparison, reproducible bit-for-bit by any third party holding the key, with no model weights and no run-to-run variance, whereas a learned localizer's decision only the model can reproduce. The comparison this paper does offer is cost and assurance: this scheme needs capture-time cooperation and a lossless container, but no training data, no GPU, no model artefact to version, deterministic $O(MN)$ cost, and exact block-level localization with a per-block audit trail. Where the acquisition pipeline is under institutional control -- medical archives, evidence management, insurance intake -- these are decisive; elsewhere the choice among alternatives is a deployment decision, not a strict dominance relation.

\textbf{AuSR3 is absent from Table~\ref{tab:baseline} deliberately.} AuSR3 \cite{ausr3} is this paper's acknowledged predecessor for the distant-mapping idea and the single most comparable same-paradigm competitor -- equally deterministic, training-free, and auditable -- but we lack its whole-image and in-region PSNR at the tamper ratios and classes evaluated here without re-implementing it, out of scope for this paper. The auditability argument this paper makes against learned localizers is, by the same logic, not available against AuSR3. What we can claim is narrower: field bindings in \eqref{eq:hmac} stated and analysed explicitly with a specific defect found and fixed; a reporting protocol that always co-reports imperceptibility, localization and recovery with $\rho$ and the unrecoverable fraction disclosed; and the block-size ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) quantifying the resolution/payload/authentication trade-off. None of this is a claim of superiority over AuSR3 on any measured metric.

\textbf{Localization granularity: an explicit trade-off, not an oversight.} The AuSR family \cite{ausr1,ausr2,ausr3} localizes at $2\times2$ block granularity, four times finer than the $8\times8$ blocks used here, and a reader familiar with that line of work will notice the difference immediately. The granularity is a consequence of payload capacity, not an independent design choice: at block size $B$ the two-LSB payload capacity is $2B^2$ bits, so $B=8$ gives 128 bits -- a 32-bit authentication tag plus a 96-bit recovery descriptor -- while $B=4$ gives only 32 bits, which a 32-bit tag alone would consume, leaving nothing for a recovery descriptor at all (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}). Finer localization therefore costs authentication strength and recovery fidelity in this design; we state this openly as a considered choice given the joint localization-\emph{and}-recovery goal of this paper, rather than presenting the coarser grid as an advantage.

\textbf{Tag width, not block size, sets the false-accept probability.} $2^{-t}$ for a $t$-bit tag depends only on $t$ (Table~\ref{tab:falseaccept}); \emph{this implementation} couples $t$ to $B$ only because its budget is a fixed $2B^2$ bits split between tag and descriptor (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}), forcing $t=16$ at $B=4$ and $t=32$ at $B=8$ -- a design that decoupled the split could hold $t$ constant at any block size. Detection is a channel-OR over three independently-keyed channels, so a three-channel tamper is missed only if all three tags collide, $2^{-3t}$: $2^{-48}$ at $B=4$, $2^{-96}$ at $B=8$, not the naive single-channel $2^{-16}$/$2^{-32}$. Predicted false accepts across the ablation grid are therefore $\approx1.6\times10^{-9}$, and the measured data agrees: zero misses of any kind. A finer grid is consequently not simply ``better localization'': it is a strictly worse authentication posture traded for sharper boundaries.

Explainability is a concrete, not rhetorical, property: the output for each block is its stored tag, its recomputed tag, and its partner block's index, compared by bitwise inequality with no threshold to tune post hoc and no saliency map requiring interpretation; for a block found unrecoverable, an explicit refusal reason is recorded rather than a silent gap. Every field in that record is reproducible identically by any third party holding the key.

\subsubsection{Threat Model}

\textbf{Adversary.} Holds the watermarked image but not $K_s$, and can apply arbitrary pixel-domain edits before verification; may know the full algorithm (Kerckhoffs's principle) -- security rests only on $K_s$. \textbf{Out of scope:} an adversary possessing $K_s$; one who edits \emph{before} embedding; lossy recompression, which this fragile-by-design scheme is not built to survive and which is a deployment-discipline requirement (lossless storage), not a cryptographic one. \textbf{Guarantee:} any modification to bit-planes 2--7 of any block is detected with probability $1-2^{-32}$ per channel (Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget}), regardless of what the adversary knows about the algorithm, as long as $K_s$ is secret. \textbf{Key compromise voids all guarantees}: an adversary holding $K_s$ can compute valid tags and descriptors for manipulated content and re-embed it so the result verifies perfectly -- the necessary consequence of a symmetric-key, deterministic construction, not a patchable defect; $K_s$ management is outside this paper's scope but not a real deployment's.

\textbf{Limitations, stated plainly.} No robustness to lossy processing whatsoever; the scheme applies only to losslessly stored imagery. Two LSB planes are permanently consumed. Localization is quantized to $8\times8$ and cannot improve without a finer grid (Table~\ref{tab:falseaccept} gives that grid's authentication cost). Recovery is low-frequency only, bounded by a 1.5~bpp budget. $\rho$ tracks $1-\alpha$ from above rather than collapsing toward it (Table~\ref{tab:rhoalpha}), but one-to-one backup allocation is still an all-or-nothing structural limit relative to reference-sharing schemes at high $\alpha$; and the block-mapping seed order must not leak structure beyond the stated $d_{\min}$ guarantee -- the quadrant-interleave order originally used for faster repair convergence did exactly this (a block's partner fell diagonally opposite 47.75\% of the time against a 29.32\% baseline) and was removed at a measured $\approx1.5$-point $\rho$ cost. The tag \eqref{eq:hmac} covers a block's complete 128-bit stored payload, so tampering with either half is detected; it does not cover whether a flagged block can be \emph{reconstructed}, which depends on its mapped partner also being intact -- a property of mapping coverage, not authentication.

\section{Conclusion and Future Scope}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a complete, deterministic, training-free framework for watermark-guided tamper localization and recovery based on self-embedding fragile watermarking. The design partitions the image into $8\times8$ blocks and allocates the 128 bits available in two LSB planes between a 32-bit keyed authentication tag stored in-block and a 96-bit compressed recovery descriptor stored in a spatially distant partner block. Computing both payloads from the six MSB planes makes the construction self-consistent without iteration; keying the tag to block index and image identifier defends against block-wise collage counterfeiting \cite{holliman2000counterfeiting}; generating the mapping as a key-seeded single-cycle permutation with enforced minimum separation implements the distant-mapping principle for avoiding the tamper coincidence problem \cite{ausr3}, guaranteeing full recoverability for attacks more compact than $d_{\min}$.

The methodological contribution is the joint evaluation protocol: over four realistic tamper classes at seven tamper ratios (10--70\%), with exact self-generated ground truth, the framework reports imperceptibility, localization precision/recall/F1/IoU, recoverability rate, and in-region recovery fidelity in a single view, making visible a trade-off the fragile-watermarking literature has often reported one half at a time. Measured over the full grid (32 images, Variants A and C, four tamper classes at seven tamper ratios, a null condition, and two ablations -- 3{,}232 rows in total, 25 of 27 automated sanity checks passing with 2 expected soft warnings), the scheme achieves block-level recall 1.000000 for three of four tamper classes and 0.999981 for object-removal-by-inpainting -- the sole class where a smooth fill can, rarely, leave a block's full stored payload untouched, 22 such blocks measured for the first time on this denser grid (block-level precision is a tautology of the block-level ground-truth rule and is not reported as a result; pixel-level precision, which does vary, averages 0.9614), zero false positives across 1.8~million null-condition block verifications -- a determinism and implementation-consistency check yielding a rule-of-three 95\% upper bound of $1.66\times10^{-6}$ on the per-block false-positive rate, not a bound on the $2^{-32}$ cryptographic false-accept probability -- and in-region recovery fidelity that stays essentially flat at 29.3--29.8~dB (mean 29.00~dB) across the entire ratio range even as whole-image quality falls with shrinking coverage $\rho$, itself tracking its structural bound $1-\alpha$ 3--6 points from above rather than collapsing toward it -- confirming coverage, not descriptor fidelity, as the governing factor on whole-image recovery, and confirming that closing the remaining gap to reference-sharing and fountain-coded schemes' graceful degradation (Future Scope item 1 below) is the highest-value next step for this design.

Target applications include digital forensic evidence archives, journalism/media authentication at ingest, medical imaging integrity in PACS, legal and official document scans, insurance claim verification, and self-authenticating camera/device firmware.

Extensions follow directly. \textbf{Reference sharing} \cite{zhang2011reference,korus2013efficient}: a shared-reference or fountain-coded allocation in place of one-to-one backup would raise the tolerable tamper ratio beyond the structural $1-\alpha$ limit -- the highest-value single upgrade. \textbf{Hierarchical multi-scale verification} \cite{lin2005hierarchical} would decouple localization resolution from per-block payload capacity. \textbf{Semi-fragile extension} \cite{linchang2000}, embedding authentication in quantization-invariant DCT relationships while retaining LSB-domain recovery data, would tolerate a bounded, declared degree of JPEG re-compression. \textbf{Reversible embedding} would allow bit-exact restoration of verified-authentic images. \textbf{Second-stage harmonic fill} for blocks still unrecoverable after \eqref{eq:recovery} is a plausible, unimplemented extension, not evaluated here and never to be merged into the primary recovery figures. \textbf{Rate-adaptive descriptors} could allocate more bits to salient blocks under a global budget. \textbf{Colour and video} would widen the recovery-data space via inter-frame mapping. \textbf{Hybrid operation with passive forensics}, using the watermark's exact mask as a trusted prior for a learned detector, combines active assurance with blind-analysis coverage.
