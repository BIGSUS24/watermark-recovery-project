@@SEC 1.1 Motivation of the Project

The evidentiary value of a digital image rests on an increasingly unsafe assumption: that the pixels presented are the pixels captured. Region-level manipulation, such as pasting an object from one photograph into another, erasing an object with an inpainting tool, or replacing a cropped region with plausible texture, is now available in free software, and diffusion-based editors require neither skill nor time. Everyday formats such as PNG, TIFF and JPEG record pixel values and protect nothing about whether they are the ones the camera or scanner produced.

The stakes are concrete. A radiological study with a lesion erased can change a diagnosis, a vehicle-damage photograph can be edited to inflate or invent an insurance loss, and a surveillance frame entered into a legal record can have a person removed from it. In each case the image is useful because of an integrity the file does not protect, and the party who relies on it usually cannot inspect the original.

Two broad families of countermeasure exist. Passive, or blind, forensics analyses the received image alone for statistical inconsistencies, and its modern instances are learned networks [2], [3]. Active approaches insert authentication information before the image is distributed, so that verification becomes a test of self-consistency rather than an inference about provenance. Fragile watermarking belongs to this second family [4], [5]. The passive detector must infer tampering from evidence the attacker may have concealed; the active scheme checks the image against information the attacker could not forge.

Fig. 1.1 contrasts the two paths. On the passive path, statistical analysis of the received image yields a heat map of suspect regions, with no restoration. On the active path, the original image is embedded with authentication and recovery data, distributed, possibly tampered with, and verified; verification localizes the altered blocks, and recovery rebuilds them from information the image itself carries. The second path is the subject of this project. It ends not at a suspicion but at either a reconstructed block or an explicit statement that the block cannot be reconstructed, and both outcomes are verifiable against the embedded data.

Even a perfect detector answers only half of the practitioner's question, since knowing which pixels of a radiograph were altered does not restore the diagnostic content that was overwritten. Passive restoration is also necessarily generative: an inpainting network fills the region with plausible content that bears no evidentiary relationship to the original. When the artifact is evidence, that is worse than no restoration, because a fabricated region that looks natural invites trust that the detector's flag was meant to withdraw. What is required is a mechanism by which the original content, even as a coarse approximation, survives the attack.

Self-embedding, introduced by Fridrich and Goljan [1], provides exactly this: a compressed representation of each region is hidden elsewhere within the same image, so that a spatially localized attack destroys a region but not its backup. This report describes a reproducible pipeline built on that principle, which evaluates localization and recovery jointly. Two further arguments bear on deployment. Learned detectors need annotated corpora and accelerator hardware that excludes embedded devices and hospital gateways, and their output is not an argument under cross-examination, since a per-pixel confidence of 0.78 cannot be recomputed or defended line by line.

The second argument concerns reporting practice. The literature habitually reports localization and recovery separately, which lets a healthy in-region reconstruction figure flatter a scheme whose recoverable fraction is undisclosed, since it describes only the blocks that happened to be recoverable. This project therefore treats the joint report of imperceptibility, localization and recovery, with the unrecoverable fraction stated openly, as a requirement of the evaluation not an optional extra.

@@SEC 1.2 Literature Survey

@@SUB 1.2.1 Passive and Active Authentication

Passive methods [2], [3] need no cooperation from the acquisition device, an advantage no active scheme can match. The price is annotated training corpora, GPU inference at run time, and restoration that is hallucinated rather than recovered. A learned detector also generalizes only as far as its training manipulations. Active watermarking inverts the robustness objective of Cox et al. [6] into fragility [4], because failure to verify is itself the authentication signal. The survey framing follows [5] and [25].

Pure fragility cannot separate a malicious splice from a benign re-save, since both disturb the embedded bits. The semi-fragile pattern [8] addresses this by tolerating compression-level change while still flagging content change. This project is deliberately fully fragile: it protects images inside a lossless custody chain, and the semi-fragile mode is treated as future scope.

@@SUB 1.2.2 Self-Embedding and Its Known Failure Modes

Fridrich and Goljan [1] are the direct ancestor of this design: block-wise compression, a key-dependent mapping of each block to a distant carrier block, and embedding in the least significant bits. Hierarchical verification [10] improves localization resolution at the cost of a higher payload per block. Security analysis [7], [9], [26] shows that a block watermark depending only on its own content lets an adversary collage a counterfeit that verifies everywhere. The authentication tag adopted here therefore binds the block content, the block index and an image-level identifier under a secret key.

A second failure mode is the tamper coincidence problem: under a one-to-one backup allocation, a block and the block that holds its backup may be destroyed together. Aminuddin and Ernawan named the problem and addressed it in AuSR3 [15] by mapping each block to the most distant available location. Their earlier AuSR1 [13] established the 2x2 recovery granularity and LSB shifting, AuSR2 [14] gave a texture-preserving variant, and [16] proposed metrics for the coincidence problem. The distant-mapping principle is theirs, not this project's.

A third line of work shares capacity across blocks. Zhang et al. [11], [19] share reference information so that reconstruction becomes the solution of a linear system, and Korus and Dziech [12] add content-adaptive allocation with a formal rate-distortion analysis. Such schemes degrade gracefully and so report far higher whole-image reconstruction at high damage than a one-to-one mapping, which fails all-or-nothing for each block. They pay with a more complex allocation and a global decoding step, whereas the one-to-one mapping adopted here keeps every block independently verifiable and every recovery a single, inspectable lookup. The limitation is real, and reference sharing is the principal upgrade path.

@@SUB 1.2.3 Learned Proactive Watermarking

A recent family embeds a learned watermark so that a trained network both localizes and recovers. EditGuard [17] jointly optimizes an invertible watermarking network for localization and copyright protection, and [18] reports a recent multi-feature fragile scheme. The statement that active watermarking detects but does not recover is therefore no longer accurate. This project's distinction is auditability: a decision here is one recomputed keyed hash, whereas a learned localizer's decision cannot be recomputed without the model weights, and its networks must be trained, versioned and run on suitable hardware. @@SUB 1.2.4 Market Survey and Research Gap

Deployed products fall into four groups, and none offers localization and recovery from inside the image. Provenance metadata is the first. The C2PA Content Credentials specification [24] attaches a signed manifest recording an asset's origin and edit history, but the manifest lives beside the pixels, so re-encoding or a metadata strip removes it, and it can never restore content. Controlled-capture services such as Truepic authenticate images at capture through a proprietary pipeline, depend on vendor infrastructure, and do not recover destroyed content.

Passive forensic tools such as Amped Authenticate and FotoForensics apply heuristics to arbitrary images, but require expert interpretation, carry no ground truth and offer no restoration. Whole-file digital signatures, as standardized for DICOM medical imaging, detect that a file has changed but neither localize the change nor recover from it. The market gap therefore mirrors the gap in the literature: existing products either attest to provenance externally or analyse pixels after the fact, and none embeds recoverable content inside the image.

The gap addressed here is deliberately narrow. The distant-mapping idea is credited to AuSR3 [15], and superiority over learned proactive watermarking such as EditGuard [17] is not claimed, since those systems already localize and recover. The contribution is threefold. First, determinism and auditability: any third party holding the key can repeat each detection bit for bit, with no training data, no GPU and no run-to-run variance. Second, an explicit keyed construction in which every bound field is analysed together with the attack it defeats. Third, joint evaluation of imperceptibility, localization and recovery, with the recoverability rate and the unrecoverable fraction disclosed rather than excluded, across four tamper classes and seven tamper ratios against exact ground-truth masks.
