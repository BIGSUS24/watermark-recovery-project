Region-level image manipulation is now free, fast and visually seamless, and the lossless formats in which evidence is normally stored, such as PNG, BMP and TIFF, protect nothing about content. Passive detectors, which analyze only the received image, answer half of the question at best: they may suggest that a region looks altered, but they cannot state which blocks were touched, cannot say what the region contained, and return no verdict that a third party can repeat. The problem addressed here is to localize tampering at block granularity and restore what was destroyed, for images that pass through a lossless custody chain.

This project presents a deterministic, training-free self-embedding fragile watermarking scheme. The image is divided into 8x8 blocks, and the two least-significant bit-planes of each block carry a 128-bit payload: a 32-bit keyed HMAC-SHA256 tag computed over the block's own six most-significant bit-planes, and a 96-bit recovery descriptor of a spatially distant partner block chosen by a key-seeded permutation. Verification recomputes each tag, and a mismatch flags the block. A flagged block is rebuilt from the descriptor its surviving partner carries; where that partner is also flagged, the block is marked unrecoverable rather than fabricated.

Across a 32-image corpus, embedding reaches a mean PSNR of 45.479 dB and SSIM of 0.98847. On the 1,792-trial main grid, block-level recall is 1.0000 for splicing, crop-and-refill and noise corruption, and 0.99998 for inpainting removal. The null condition, 1,802,240 verifications of unmodified images, produced no false-positive block. The recoverability rate sits 3 to 6 percentage points above its structural bound of 1 - alpha at every tamper ratio, falling from 0.9590 at 10% tamper to 0.3579 at 70%, and in-region recovery quality is 28.49 dB for Variant A and 31.13 dB for Variant C. What distinguishes the design is accountability rather than accuracy: every decision is a single recomputed keyed hash that any third party holding the key can repeat bit for bit.
